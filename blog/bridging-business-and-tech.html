Bridging Business and Tech: Product Requirements Your Developers Can Build

Many startups burn months building the wrong features. The gap is rarely technical skill. It is the translation between how the business works and what the tech team is asked to build.

Riishi KalawatiaNovember 2026 · 6 min read

These lessons come from startup roles working closely with CTOs and technology teams on product requirements and first user testing. The perspective is that of an operations leader, not a developer. That turned out to be an advantage: it meant explaining how the business actually works on the ground, and testing whether the product worked for the people who would use it every day.

1. Start with the real user flow, not the feature list

Before writing a single requirement, walk through the real journey: a customer placing an order, a seller listing a product, a warehouse picker fulfilling it, a support agent handling a complaint. Write each step as it happens today, including the workarounds. Features should come from this flow, not from a competitor's app.

2. Write requirements in business language

A good requirement says who needs to do what, why, and how you will know it works. For example: “A store manager needs to see items running below minimum stock before the evening peak, so that shelves are refilled in time.” Developers can design the best technical solution once they understand the business outcome.

3. Agree on what is not in version one

Most delays come from scope that keeps growing. Decide with your CTO what the first release must do, and what can wait. A smaller product that works in real conditions is worth more than a large one stuck in development.

4. Test with real people in real conditions

First user testing should happen on the warehouse floor, in the store, or with actual sellers and customers, not only in a meeting room. Watch where people hesitate, where they get stuck, and where they invent shortcuts. Those moments are your most valuable feedback.

5. Keep operations in the room after launch

Technology and operations should review the product together regularly after launch. Operations teams see problems first; tech teams can fix them fastest when they hear about them early.

The takeaway

Founders and SME owners do not need to learn to code to build great products. They need someone who can translate the business into clear requirements and test the result in the real world. That bridge is often the difference between a product that launches and a product that works.
