Cold Chain Is a Promise: What Online Grocery Gets Wrong

Customers never see your cold chain. They only see whether the milk is cold and the vegetables are fresh. Here is how to keep that promise at scale.

Riishi KalawatiaNovember 2026 · 6 min read

When I ran warehouse operations at bigbasket, our team was recognised with the 2nd rank pan-India for cold chain operations. The award was nice. What mattered more was what it taught me: a cold chain is not equipment. It is a promise you make to a customer you will never meet, and you keep it or break it hundreds of times a day.

Online grocery is growing in almost every market, from Indian metros to the Gulf, Southeast Asia, Europe and the Americas. The business models differ, from scheduled slots to 10-minute delivery, but the cold chain problems are remarkably similar everywhere.

1. The weakest link is almost never the freezer

Companies invest heavily in cold rooms and refrigerated vehicles, then lose temperature in the two places nobody watches: the dock and the last few metres. A pallet left on an open dock while the team finishes another task, or a tote that waits at the door while a picker looks for the last item, does more damage than an old compressor.

The fix is process, not capital. Set a maximum time any chilled or frozen item may spend outside a controlled zone, make it visible on the floor, and make someone own it on every shift.

2. Pick cold items last, pack them first

Pick sequencing is one of the cheapest cold chain tools available. Ambient goods are picked first; chilled and frozen items are picked last, packed into insulated bags or with gel packs immediately, and loaded last so they come off first. It sounds obvious. In practice it has to be designed into the warehouse layout and the picking system, or it will not survive a busy evening.

3. Slots and speed change the risk, not the rule

Slot-based delivery gives you time to plan routes and loading; ultra-fast delivery gives you less time in transit but more handling under pressure. Both work if the turnaround time you promise matches the cold chain you can actually run. The worst outcome is promising a delivery window your packaging cannot protect.

4. Measure what the customer experiences

Temperature logs in the cold room are necessary, but they are not the customer's experience. Track product-quality complaints, returns and refunds by category, by store or dark store, and by delivery slot. Patterns appear quickly: a particular route, a particular shift, a particular product family. That is where your cold chain is really breaking.

5. Train for judgement, not just compliance

The best cold chain teams I have worked with do not just follow a checklist. They understand why the checklist exists, so when something unusual happens, such as a vehicle breakdown or a sudden spike in orders, they make the right call without waiting for a manager.

The takeaway

Whether you deliver groceries in Bengaluru, Dubai or Kingston, the principle is the same: design the process so that the cold item is never the one that waits. Customers will never thank you for a cold chain that works. They will simply keep ordering, and that is the only recognition that counts.
