15 Red Flags in Hotel and Resort Purchasing

Most food and beverage cost leaks start long before the kitchen. They start in purchase orders, invoices and store transfers that nobody checks.

Riishi KalawatiaNovember 2026 · 7 min read

When I review the operations of a hotel or resort, I rarely start in the kitchen. I start with the paperwork: purchase orders, supplier invoices and store transfers. Food and beverage cost problems almost always leave a trail there first, long before they show up as a bad month in the P&L.

My method is simple. Every transaction should pass a control. Every control should be validated with data. Every exception the data throws up should be explained. That is where the insight, and the saving, usually sits. Here are fifteen red flags I look for, grouped by where they appear.

Purchase orders

1. Orders posted days after the purchase. If a purchase order is raised after the goods arrive, it is not a control, it is a record. Orders should be posted before or within 24 hours of the commitment.

2. Prices above the negotiated rate. Compare every order line with the agreed supplier cost. Small differences on high-volume items add up quickly across a year.

3. Orders to vendors who are not approved, or are blocked. If the system allows it, someone will eventually do it. Restrict ordering to the approved vendor list.

4. Items that are not in the item master. Free-text items bypass pricing, stock and recipe controls. Every regularly bought item needs a code.

5. Odd quantities and wrong units of measure. An order for 7.3 cases, or kilograms where the supplier sells by case, is often a sign of a keying error or a mismatch between what is ordered and what is received.

6. Gaps in the order number sequence. Missing or cancelled numbers should be explainable. Unexplained gaps are a classic warning sign.

Supplier invoices and receiving

7. Invoices without the receiving stamp or signature. No proof of receipt should mean no payment. This one check stops a surprising amount of leakage.

8. Short or excess deliveries accepted silently. Every shortage or excess should be acknowledged in writing and reflected in the invoice before it is posted.

9. Pack-size mismatches. If the supplier's case size differs from the case size in your system, your stock and cost figures are wrong from day one.

10. Stock bought but never sold or used. Slow-moving and non-moving items, and discontinued products that are still being ordered, tie up cash and end up as waste.

Store transfers and production

11. Transfers without an approved requisition. Goods leaving the main store for the kitchen or bar should always have a signed request and a signed acknowledgement.

12. Transfers larger than the request. If the kitchen asked for 10 kilograms and 15 were issued, the extra five usually disappear from the records.

13. Consumption that does not match the recipe. Standard recipes and portion sizes let you calculate what should have been used for what was sold. The gap is your real shrinkage.

14. Daily waste that is not recorded. Waste happens in every kitchen. Unrecorded waste turns an operational problem into an unexplained loss.

15. Shortages in peak season. Running out during peak occupancy is often a planning problem, not a supplier problem. Par levels and forecasts based on guest bookings prevent it.

From red flags to better decisions

None of these checks needs expensive software. Most can start as a weekly exception report from the system you already have, reviewed by someone who has the authority to act. Once the basics are under control, the same data supports bigger improvements: supplier cost tracking, booking-based forecasting, barcoding high-value items and monitoring energy and water use.

The takeaway

Cost control in hospitality is not about cutting quality. It is about making sure that every rupee, dollar or dirham you spend on food and beverage reaches the guest's plate. Whether your property is in India, the Caribbean or the Gulf, these fifteen questions are a practical place to start.
