One Network, Many Models: Fulfillment for D2C, B2C and B2B Across Borders

Brands now sell direct, through marketplaces and to businesses at the same time. Fulfillment has to serve all three without building three separate operations.

Riishi KalawatiaNovember 2026 · 6 min read

A decade ago, most brands chose one route to market. Today a single company may sell direct to consumers on its own website, through several marketplaces, to retailers and distributors in bulk, and increasingly across borders. Each channel has different order sizes, packaging, service levels and paperwork.

At Garage, where I lead global fulfillment, the platform is built around this reality: D2C, B2C, B2B and white-label models running on one network. Here is what I have learned about making that work.

1. Separate the inventory view from the channel view

The most common mistake is letting each channel keep its own stock. It feels safe, but it leads to stock-outs in one channel while another sits on excess. One shared, accurate inventory pool, with clear allocation rules per channel, is the foundation of multi-model fulfillment.

2. Standardise the core, flex the edges

Receiving, quality checks, storage and picking should run the same way regardless of where the order came from. What changes is the edge: a consumer parcel needs branded packaging and fast dispatch; a business order needs pallets, invoices and delivery appointments. Design one core process with channel-specific steps at the end, not separate processes from the start.

3. Quality control is a brand decision

In a white-label model, the customer sees your brand on the box, not the supplier's. That means inbound quality checks and repackaging are not back-office tasks; they are how your brand is experienced. Every unit that reaches a customer should have passed a check you would be comfortable putting your name on.

4. Cross-border adds paperwork, not a new business

Selling internationally introduces customs documentation, duties, longer transit times and different return expectations. These are real challenges, but they are manageable with the right partners and clear data. The mistake is treating international as a separate company; the better approach is to extend the same network, inventory and quality standards with country-specific compliance layered on top.

5. Make the last mile a partnership

Few companies own their last-mile delivery in every market, and few should. Choose delivery partners carefully, integrate them properly, and measure them on the customer's experience: on-time delivery, damage rates and how returns are handled.

The takeaway

The network economy rewards companies that can serve every customer type without multiplying cost. One network, many models: shared inventory, a standard core, brand-grade quality control and strong partners. That is how fulfillment becomes a growth engine instead of a cost centre.
